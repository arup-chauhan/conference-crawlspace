# Explaining conference material

Use this workflow when the user wants to understand talks, learn their ideas, or turn conference material into an illustrated report or deck.

## Source briefs before storyboarding

For each selected talk record:

- Identity: exact title, speaker, edition, and source origin.
- Evidence inspected: slides and page numbers, recording timestamps, transcript, or abstract only.
- Problem and starting architecture: what was difficult and why it mattered.
- Mechanism: what changed, who acts, what state or data moves, and the causal steps.
- Outcome: reported result with its baseline and conditions, or no result available.
- Constraints: prerequisites, correctness boundaries, unfinished work, and uncertainties.
- Connection: which user interest it addresses and how it relates to another source.

Read the source body; do not build these briefs from titles alone. Inspect diagrams and chart labels visually where they carry the explanation. Sparse panel slides do not establish what panelists said. Community agendas distinguish proposed topics, actual discussion, and adopted changes; future agenda entries are not completed meetings.

Supplement missing details with official technical documentation when useful. Label it as supplementary explanation, not content verified in that talk. Search for alternate public recordings or speaker material before declaring a gap. Preserve gaps when no substantive material is available.

## Simple models with accurate mechanisms

Use a concrete example or analogy to reduce unfamiliar concepts. Map the analogy back to actual components. Explain the boundary: where the model simplifies behavior, and what it does not establish. Keep invented example values visibly illustrative.

A teaching unit usually needs a problem, an illustration of the mechanism in a few causal steps, and one implication or limitation. Choose the visual to explain a relationship, movement, sequence, or tradeoff. Decorative infrastructure artwork alone does not explain an architecture. Animation should progressively reveal the mechanism while preserving a readable complete state.

State the individual source contribution before synthesizing across talks. A cross-source architecture is an interpretation unless a source explicitly presents that whole system. Treat speaker performance claims as reported, preserve denominators and workload conditions, and do not turn draft figures into established findings.

## Scope and completion

Map agreed topics to relevant talks and source briefs. Broad requests need coverage across that inventory, not a token shortlist. Put lower-evidence items in an explicit coverage section; do not give them the same weight as inspected decks.

Before delivery, assess:

- Does the artifact explain what the covered speakers actually did?
- Can a reader describe how each main mechanism works without following the source link?
- Do illustrations teach the mechanism rather than decorate it?
- Are source findings, explanatory analogies, cross-source inferences, and proposed experiments distinguishable?
- Do the remaining gaps accurately describe the material that was unavailable?

For a learning request, recommendations and experiments may follow the teaching. They must not displace it.
